Data Classification: A Practical Guide from Public to Restricted

Classify data into four levels, decide with the right criteria, and attach handling rules that make the labels mean something.

ArticleUpdated 2026-11-05
On this page

  • Four levels are enough
  • The criteria behind the decision
  • Make the label mean something
  • Common mistakes
  • A rollout that survives contact with reality

Data classification is the act of deciding how sensitive each piece of information is, writing that decision down, and attaching rules to it. It sounds bureaucratic and becomes useless when it is: a scheme nobody follows is worse than no scheme, because it creates the illusion that data is controlled. This guide is the minimum version that actually works.

Why bother

  • Incident scope. When something leaks, the first question is "what was in there?" Classification answers it in minutes instead of weeks.
  • Cost. Not everything needs the same encryption, backup tier or access review. Classification stops you paying enterprise prices for public marketing copy.
  • Obligation. Privacy law, contracts and industry standards all ask you to know what personal and confidential data you hold and where it lives.

Four levels are enough

LevelTestTypical examples
PublicCould be published on the website with no harm.Marketing copy, public documentation, published prices.
InternalHarmless outside the company in the short term, but not intended for release.Meeting notes, internal wiki, staff directory, non-sensitive project plans.
ConfidentialWould cause real damage to people, customers or the business if disclosed.Customer contracts, source code, financial reports before publication, personal data of employees.
RestrictedRegulated, contractual, or catastrophic if disclosed. Access is need-to-know and logged.Health records, payment data, credentials and keys, security incident details, merger documents.

More levels than four is usually a taxonomy problem disguised as a policy. If two levels are always treated the same way in practice, they are one level.

The criteria behind the decision

Classify by the harm of disclosure first, then check the other three:

  • Harm. Who is hurt, and how badly, if this is public tomorrow?
  • Legal or contractual duty. Does a regulation, standard or customer contract name this data type? GDPR personal data, PCI card data and HIPAA records are effectively pre-classified.
  • Aggregation. Individually harmless records become confidential in bulk. A single employee's salary is confidential; the whole payroll file is restricted.
  • Time. Most classifications expire. Financial results are restricted until published, then public. Build the expiry into the label.

Make the label mean something

A label with no handling rule is decoration. For each level, define storage, sharing, retention and deletion:

StorageSharingAccess review
PublicAnyOpenNone
InternalCompany systemsStaff with a work reasonAnnual
ConfidentialApproved systems, encryptedNamed individuals, no external forwardingQuarterly
RestrictedApproved systems, encrypted, access loggedNamed individuals, approved per caseMonthly

Common mistakes

  • Classifying everything restricted. It is the safe answer and it destroys the point of classifying. If everything is critical, triage becomes impossible.
  • Classifying the container, not the content. A "public" shared drive containing one contract is not public.
  • No owner. Every classified dataset needs one named person who decides who gets access.
  • No tooling. If applying a label takes three manual steps, nobody will apply it. Labels must attach where the data is created — the document editor, the database column, the bucket policy.
  • No review. Data ages. A project folder from 2019 still marked confidential is probably archive material, and treating it as live costs attention.

A rollout that survives contact with reality

  1. Pick the four levels and their handling rules. One page, not a policy document.
  2. Inventory where data actually lives: systems, shared drives, SaaS tools, databases.
  3. Pilot on one dataset with a real owner — payroll, customer support tickets or the code repository.
  4. Label it, apply the rules, and measure what breaks. Something will.
  5. Publish the scheme, then extend dataset by dataset. Do not wait for a perfect inventory.
  6. Review quarterly, and delete the classification of anything nobody has opened in a year.
