FIDO2 vs Passkeys: What's the Difference?

"Turn on FIDO2." "Use a passkey." "Get a security key." The advice pages use the words as if they were synonyms, and half the vendor pages do too. They are not the same thing - and the difference decides what happens the day you lose your laptop. Here is the honest version: what FIDO2 actually is, what a passkey actually is, and which one belongs on which account.

The one-sentence difference

Every passkey is a FIDO2 credential, but not every FIDO2 credential is a passkey. FIDO2 is the protocol family - the plumbing. A passkey is a product concept built on that plumbing: a discoverable credential, usually synced across your devices. A YubiKey that holds a key which never leaves the hardware is FIDO2 too, but nobody calls it a passkey.

What FIDO2 actually is

FIDO2 is two specifications working together, published by the FIDO Alliance and the W3C:

  • WebAuthn - the browser API a website calls to create and verify credentials. If you build a login page, this is the part you implement. WebAuthn Level 3 became a W3C Recommendation on 25 August 2026.
  • CTAP2 (Client to Authenticator Protocol) - how the browser talks to an external authenticator: a USB security key, a phone. The current revision, CTAP2.2, formalizes the hybrid transport (QR code plus Bluetooth proximity) used for cross-device sign-in.

The core move is the same in every variant: your device creates a fresh public/private key pair per site, the site stores only the public key, and sign-in is a signed challenge instead of a shared secret. There is no password to leak, reuse, or hand to a phishing page - the credential is cryptographically bound to the real domain.

What a passkey actually is

A passkey is a discoverable (resident) FIDO2 credential - the authenticator stores the credential and the account it belongs to, so it can offer "your account on this site" without the server sending credential IDs first. That is what makes username-less, one-tap sign-in work.

The second property matters more in daily life: passkeys are usually synced. Apple replicates them through iCloud Keychain, Google through Password Manager, Microsoft through your Windows/cloud account, and password managers (1Password, Bitwarden, Dashlane) sync them across platforms. Lose your phone and the passkey is on the replacement. The price: the key material now lives with the provider that syncs it - its account security and recovery process become part of your threat model.

The real decision: synced versus device-bound

FIDO2-versus-passkey is not a decision you make; synced-versus-device-bound is. And it turns on exactly one question: what happens when the device is lost?

  • Synced passkey - the credential travels with your provider account. Recovery is your provider's problem (and their account recovery is now your weakest link, which is why the email account behind it deserves the strongest protection you own).
  • Device-bound key - the credential dies with the hardware. The mitigation is boring and effective: register at least two authenticators and store them separately.

Since WebAuthn Level 2, a relying party can even tell the difference: assertions carry backup-eligible and backup-state flags, so a security-conscious site can require device-bound credentials where policy demands it.

Phishing resistance: where both win

Against phishing - still the number-one way in - both variants are the same victory. The private key only answers challenges from the genuine domain's origin, so a pixel-perfect fake login page gets a signature for a different origin, or nothing at all. Your fingerprint or PIN never leaves the device; it only unlocks the local key store. This is the property that matters more than any label: a TOTP code can be typed into a fake page in real time, a passkey or security key signature cannot.

What to use where

  • Everyday accounts (email, social, shopping) - passkeys. The friction-free option that actually gets enabled.
  • The accounts that guard everything else (primary email, cloud, password manager, source control) - passkeys plus a registered hardware security key as backup. The passkey glossary entry keeps this checklist short.
  • Admin and break-glass access - device-bound security keys with attestation. When policy must prove the key never left hardware, only a security key can.
  • Legacy fallback - where passkeys are unsupported, app-based TOTP beats SMS 2FA.

Frequently asked

What is the difference between FIDO2, WebAuthn and passkeys?

FIDO2 is the umbrella: WebAuthn (the W3C browser API) plus CTAP (the protocol to external authenticators). A passkey is a specific kind of FIDO2 credential - discoverable and usually synced. You implement WebAuthn; the user ends up holding a passkey or a security key.

Is a passkey the same as a WebAuthn credential?

Every passkey is a WebAuthn credential, but not every WebAuthn credential is a passkey. Earlier WebAuthn deployments used non-discoverable credentials (the server supplied credential IDs); a passkey is discoverable - the authenticator remembers the account itself.

Are passkeys just biometric login?

No. The biometric (Face ID, Touch ID, Windows Hello) only unlocks the local key store - like a screen lock for the credential. The credential is a cryptographic key pair, and your fingerprint or face never leaves the device.

Do passkeys replace hardware security keys?

For most people and accounts, yes. For privileged and regulated access, no: device-bound keys support attestation and AAGUID allowlisting, and a private key that provably never left hardware is a stronger guarantee than any sync story.

What happens if I lose my phone with my passkeys?

If they are synced, sign in on any other device in the same provider account and they are there. If they were device-bound on a lost key and you registered no second authenticator, that account is gone until the site's recovery process lets you back in - which is exactly why you register two.

Can passkeys be phished?

The credential cannot be - it only signs challenges for the genuine origin, so a fake page gets nothing usable. What attackers phish instead is the recovery path: the email and provider accounts that can mint a new passkey. Protect those first.
