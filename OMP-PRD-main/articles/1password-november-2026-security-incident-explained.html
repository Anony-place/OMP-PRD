1Password November 2026 Incident: What Leaked and What Zero-Knowledge Protected

1Password investigated a November 19, 2026 intrusion tracing back to a compromised corporate GitHub account. Here is what non-secret metadata was accessed, why…

ArticleUpdated 2026-11-26
On this page
  • Timeline and how the November 2026 intrusion happened
  • What data was exposed vs. what stayed encrypted
  • Why zero-knowledge architecture held up under server compromise
  • Do you need to rotate every password inside your vault?
  • The developer takeaway: CI/CD identity is tier-zero

In late November 2026, password manager provider 1Password disclosed a backend security incident originating from an employee's compromised corporate GitHub account. Whenever a major password manager reports a breach, the immediate fear is whether stored logins were exposed. Understanding the strict boundary between account metadata and zero-knowledge vault ciphertext explains why customer passwords remained safe—and why targeted phishing risk rises sharply right after a breach disclosure.

Timeline and how the November 2026 intrusion happened

1Password initiated an investigation on November 19, 2026 after detecting unauthorized activity tied to an employee's corporate GitHub credentials. According to company disclosures:

  • Initial developer access: The threat actor leveraged the compromised corporate GitHub account to access private repositories and push unauthorized code into 1Password backend services and CI/CD build workflows.
  • Data exfiltration window: The injected code targeted administrative records and downloaded account metadata for a subset of customer accounts before containment cut off access.
  • Containment & pipeline hardening: 1Password revoked the compromised tokens, restored clean service artifacts, rotated potentially exposed internal infrastructure secrets, and hardened developer endpoint and CI/CD controls.

What data was exposed vs. what stayed encrypted

Data categoryExposed in Nov 2026 incident?Security impact for users
Vault items, passwords, TOTP seeds & passkeysNo — never accessed or decryptedProtected end-to-end by your Master Password and 128-bit Secret Key (derived locally).
Account names & sign-in emailsYes (subset of users)High risk of follow-on spear phishing mimicking support or billing emails.
User/account IDs & sign-in timestampsYes (subset of users)Non-secret metadata; useful for attacker lure crafting, cannot unlock a vault.
Public keys & SRP parametersYes (subset of users)Protocol values required during authentication handshake; insufficient on their own to access an account.
Short-lived invitation/recovery email tokensYes (small subset mid-process)Directly notified by 1Password; unusable alone without secondary verification (SSO or verification codes). As hardening, codes expanded from 6 numeric digits to 10 alphanumeric characters.

Why zero-knowledge architecture held up under server compromise

As detailed in our guide on whether password managers are safe, zero-knowledge managers never upload your master key or plaintext vault to vendor servers. 1Password derives its decryption key from two inputs combined strictly on your device:

  1. Your Master Password (memorized by you, never sent across the network).
  2. Your Secret Key (stored only on your authorized devices and emergency kit, adding cryptographic entropy even if the backend password-derived verifier were ever attacked offline).

Even with unauthorized execution inside backend services, an attacker without your local Master Password and Secret Key only sees encrypted blobs—and in this incident, 1Password reported encrypted vaults were not even copied.

Do you need to rotate every password inside your vault?

1Password stated that standard customers do not need a blanket reset of stored passwords or Master Passphrases because vault encryption keys remained untouched. However, security-conscious users—especially anyone who received a direct notification about mid-flight invitation/recovery emails or whose sign-in email matched company domains—should follow four pragmatic precautions:

  • Audit every 1Password email carefully: Expect attackers to replay breached customer email lists into lookalike "urgent account verification" phishing campaigns. Inspect links locally with the Phishing URL Scanner before clicking.
  • Verify Master Password uniqueness: If your Master Password was ever reused elsewhere, generate a strong unique passphrase using our Passphrase Generator and verify strength locally on the Password Strength Checker.
  • Confirm Secret Key & Emergency Kit hygiene: Make sure your Emergency Kit is stored offline rather than emailed in plaintext or left in an unencrypted cloud folder.
  • Check if your email appears in prior breaches: Understand full exposure across unrelated breaches via our Data Breach Response Guide and k-Anonymity Breach Check.

The developer takeaway: CI/CD identity is tier-zero

Just like automated attacks against misconfigured developer tooling, a single compromised corporate GitHub token can bypass runtime network perimeters cleanly through trusted build pipelines. Enforce hardware-backed FIDO2 / passkey MFA on source repositories, require signed commits with branch protection approvals, keep CI secrets ephemeral with short OIDC leases instead of static keys, and scan commits continuously using tools covered in our TruffleHog guide.
