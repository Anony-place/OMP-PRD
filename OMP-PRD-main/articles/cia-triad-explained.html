CIA Triad Explained: Confidentiality, Integrity & Availability

Three words run under every security program, every certification exam, and every post-incident review: confidentiality, integrity, availability. The CIA triad is the first model most people in security learn and the one they never stop using - not because it is deep, but because it is complete enough to sort every failure into one of three buckets. Here is what each property means, how real attacks map onto the triad, and - because the model has real limits - where it stops being enough.

The three properties

Confidentiality - keeping secrets secret

Information is read only by the people and systems authorized to read it. Encryption, access control, data classification, and the habit of not collecting what you do not need are all confidentiality controls. The canonical failure: a database of customer records published on a paste site.

Integrity - data and code stay trustworthy

Information is changed only through authorized, correct paths - and can prove it. Checksums, signatures, version control, input validation, and audit logs are integrity controls. The canonical failure: transaction amounts altered in the database, or an update server serving a tampered installer.

Availability - reachable when needed

Systems and data are accessible to authorized users at the moment they need them. Redundancy, backups, capacity planning, DDoS mitigation, and patching are availability controls. The canonical failure: ransomware encrypting the file share, or a denial-of-service knocking the storefront offline.

This mapping is the triad's real job: in an incident review, "what property did we lose?" immediately implies the controls that failed and the ones to fix. Losing availability points at backups and resilience; losing confidentiality points at access control and encryption; losing integrity points at verification and signing.

Where the model came from

The triad has no clean single origin story. Confidentiality was formalized first - early computing was defense computing, and secrecy was the whole problem. Integrity became a commercial priority when banks needed proof that electronic records were not silently altered. Availability entered the vocabulary with the first denial-of-service incidents. The tidy three-letter name consolidated later, and by the late 1990s "the CIA triad" was standard classroom language. Understanding that it emerged from practice, not from one paper, explains why it maps so well onto real incidents.

Using the triad as a checklist

For any asset - a database, an API, a laptop - three questions:

  • Confidentiality: who can read this today, and does that list still make sense?
  • Integrity: who can change this, and would we be able to tell?
  • Availability: what breaks if this disappears for an hour, and what is the recovery time?

Controls often serve more than one property, and the interesting cases are where they trade off: full-disk encryption protects confidentiality but a lost key destroys availability (and utility); strict change control protects integrity but slows the availability of fixes. Naming the trade-off out loud beats discovering it during an outage.

Where the triad falls short

Three buckets are not six. The classic counter-example: a stolen laptop with a fully encrypted drive. Confidentiality is intact - the data is unreadable. But something was lost: you no longer control the copy. In 1998, Donn B. Parker proposed the Parkerian hexad for exactly these gaps, adding three properties that can be lost independently:

  • Possession (control) - the encrypted-laptop case.
  • Authenticity - the message is intact and confidential but forged at the source.
  • Utility - the data survives but is useless to you (the backup format nobody can read anymore).

The hexad is not a replacement - it is the triad with finer grain for forensics and awkward cases. Learn the three first; reach for the six when an incident does not fit.

Frequently asked

What does the C in the CIA triad stand for?

Confidentiality - keeping information readable only by authorized parties. The other two letters are Integrity and Availability.

What is the CIA triad in simple terms?

A three-part definition of "secure": the data stays secret from the wrong people (confidentiality), stays uncorrupted and trustworthy (integrity), and stays reachable for the right people (availability).

Which CIA triad principle does ransomware violate?

Primarily availability - MITRE maps the encryption step to T1486 "Data Encrypted for Impact", an availability attack. When the crew also exfiltrates and leaks data (double extortion), confidentiality falls too.

What is an example of a CIA triad breach?

A leaked customer database: confidentiality lost (records exposed), often integrity lost too (records altered or deleted), and availability lost if the database is taken offline. Sorting each loss separately is what makes the response plan concrete.

What is the Parkerian hexad?

Donn B. Parker's 1998 extension of the triad, adding possession (control), authenticity, and utility - the three things that can go wrong even when confidentiality, integrity, and availability technically hold.

Is the CIA triad outdated?

No - it remains the working model for most security programs because nearly every real failure lands in one of its three buckets. It is incomplete rather than obsolete; the hexad and modern frameworks add grain, they do not replace it.
