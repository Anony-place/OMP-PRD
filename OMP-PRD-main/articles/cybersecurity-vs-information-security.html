Cybersecurity vs Information Security: What's the Difference?

People use 'cybersecurity' and 'information security' as if they were the same thing, and in most job descriptions they are. But when you are scoping a policy, an audit, or a compliance regime, the difference between the two words decides what you actually protect. Here is what each term means, what the standards say, and how to tell which one you are looking at.

The one-sentence difference

Information security protects information itself, wherever it lives. Cybersecurity protects the digital systems, networks and services that store, process and move it. The difference is scope, not intent - both fields are trying to keep the same promises.

That split has one useful consequence: every cybersecurity incident is an information security incident, but not the other way around. A printed patient file left in a taxi is an information-security failure with no malware anywhere near it. A ransomware crew encrypting the file share is both.

What the standards actually say

The NIST glossary defines cybersecurity (from CNSSI 4009-2022) as the 'prevention of damage to, protection of, and restoration of computers, electronic communications systems, electronic communications services... including information contained therein, to ensure its availability, integrity, authentication, confidentiality, and nonrepudiation.' Notice the wording: the systems are the object of protection, and the information inside them is included.

The same NIST entry carries a history note worth reading twice: the term 'cybersecurity' replaced 'information assurance' in US government policy in 2014, and 'information assurance' had earlier replaced 'information security' and 'computer security' in the same way - while 'information security and computer security are still used... depending on scope and intent.' The vocabulary has been reshuffled for policy reasons, not because the problems changed.

On the other side, the international standard for managing information security is ISO/IEC 27001:2022, whose full title is Information security, cybersecurity and privacy protection - Information security management systems - Requirements. ISO stacks all three words in one title because an information security management system (ISMS) has to cover the data, the digital estate around it, and the privacy obligations attached to it. The standard is explicit that information security covers information 'in all forms, including paper-based, cloud-based and digital data'.

So the clean mental model: information security is the discipline; cybersecurity is the part of it that happens on and around connected technology.

Where they overlap - and where they split

The overlap is the goal. Both fields defend the same three properties - confidentiality, integrity and availability - which is why the CIA triad shows up in both conversations. The split is the layer where each control lives:

  • Physical controls (locked server rooms, clean desks, shredding) are pure information security.
  • Technical controls (encryption, endpoint hardening, network segmentation, identity providers) are where the two fields meet.
  • Operational controls (who may approve a change, how incidents escalate, how data is classified and retired) are information security processes that cyber systems enforce.

A locked server room is an information-security measure expressed in a cyber environment. A data classification policy is information security deciding what matters; the access-control rules that implement it are cybersecurity. Asking 'does this control protect the information, or only the system that serves it?' is the fastest way to see which side of the line you are on.

Three real-world cases that show the boundary

An ISO 27001 audit is an information-security exercise that necessarily covers the cyber systems holding the information. Auditors scope the engagement around information assets and risks, then test the technology where that information lives - which is why 'the ISO audit' and 'the security review' feel like the same week of work.

A data-protection regulator looks at a subset. Privacy law covers personal data specifically - a slice of everything an organisation treats as information. A breach of engineering source code can be a serious information-security incident with no personal-data notification duty at all; a leaked customer list is both.

A lost company laptop is both a physical-security loss and an information-security loss, and the response depends on which data was on it - which is the classification question again. The cyber angle (full-disk encryption, remote wipe) is a control, not the incident category.

Which one do you need?

For almost every practical decision, you need both words in the room, scoped properly:

  • Write policies against the data and services you care about, not against the tool you happen to own. 'Protect customer records' outlives any vendor; 'configure the firewall' does not.
  • Map each regulatory obligation to the specific information it covers - privacy law to personal data, payment rules to cardholder data, and so on. The obligation's scope is the information, not the network.
  • Keep physical, operational and technical controls in one register so the gaps between them stay visible. Most real incidents live in the gaps: the backup that existed but was never restored, the laptop that was encrypted but with a taped-on password.

And when the work turns into testing - finding what is actually broken across systems and data together - that is vulnerability assessment versus penetration testing territory, where scope decisions matter in exactly the same way.

What it means for job titles

In hiring, the two terms are close to interchangeable: 'security analyst', 'cybersecurity analyst' and 'information security analyst' describe overlapping work, and postings drift with fashion rather than with any formal taxonomy (the NIST terminology note says as much). If you are choosing a career path, learn the shared core first - risk, the CIA triad, identity, networks, and how real attacks actually work - and let the job title worry about itself. For what the roles pay, see our cyber security salary in India breakdown (and the USA guide).

If your interest is the offensive side - legally testing systems the way real attackers do, within a defined scope - that discipline has its own rules and boundaries, covered in bug bounty and responsible disclosure.

FAQ

Is cybersecurity part of information security, or the other way around?

Cybersecurity is best understood as the part of information security that deals with connected technology - systems, networks and services. Information security is broader because it also covers information on paper, in people's heads, and in physical processes. The standards overlap deliberately; the words describe scope, not rival camps.

Cybersecurity vs information security - which is bigger?

Information security is the wider discipline: it protects data in every form. Cybersecurity covers the digital estate within it. A useful test: if the control would still matter with the internet switched off - locking cabinets, classifying documents, vetting staff - it is information security.

Are the two terms the same in job descriptions?

Mostly, yes. NIST's own glossary notes that the terms have replaced each other in policy language over the decades while both remain in use 'depending on scope and intent'. Compare the responsibilities in a posting, not the headline.

Do I need a separate information security policy and a cybersecurity policy?

You need one coherent set of policies scoped to your information and services. Splitting them into two disconnected documents creates exactly the gaps attackers use. One risk register, one control set, one incident process - with clear owners for the physical, human and technical parts.

What should I learn first?

Learn the shared core: the CIA triad, risk, identity and access, networks, encryption basics and how attacks work. Then specialise. Our CIA triad guide is the right first page, and web application security basics is the right second one for the digital side.
