Bug Bounty vs Responsible Disclosure: How to Report Bugs Legally

Finding a vulnerability is the easy part. Knowing whether you are allowed to look, who to tell, and what protects you afterwards - that is the part people get wrong. This guide explains how bug bounties and responsible disclosure actually work, where the legal lines are, and how to write a report a security team can act on.

The one-sentence difference

A bug bounty pays for findings; responsible disclosure is the same activity without the payment. Both are legitimate, and both depend on the same foundation: a defined scope and a real safe harbour. Without those, you are not a researcher - you are someone testing systems you were never authorised to touch.

How a disclosure programme actually works

A programme - whether a paid bounty or a plain vulnerability disclosure policy (VDP) - is a published statement of the rules of engagement. Read it before you type anything:

  • Scope lists what you may test (which domains, apps, APIs) and what is excluded. Treat anything not named as excluded.
  • Prohibited actions usually include denial of service, social engineering of staff, and automated scanning outside a rate limit.
  • Safe harbour is the promise that good-faith research within the rules will not be pursued legally. Without it, every researcher is gambling.
  • Severity is set by impact, not cleverness - programmes triage reports by what an attacker could actually achieve.
  • Duplicates and out-of-scope submissions are normal. The first reporter of a common bug gets the bounty; everyone else gets a polite duplicate. Set expectations before you start.

The CISA directive BOD 20-01 is the clearest public statement of what a proper VDP contains. Since September 2020 it has required every US federal civilian executive branch agency to publish one - a policy describing 'the activities that can be undertaken in order to find and report vulnerabilities in a legally authorized manner', plus handling procedures so reports actually get answered. CISA's framing is blunt about why: disclosure is 'the act of initially providing vulnerability information to a party that was not believed to be previously aware' - and without a published policy, the people who would help get turned away.

Safe harbour - the part that decides everything

Most anti-hacking laws were written before 'hacking for good' existed as a routine activity, and they have been used against researchers acting in good faith. Safe harbour is policy language that fixes this: if you act in good faith, within scope, and report what you find, the organisation commits not to pursue you.

The disclose.io project maintains the standard, lawyer-reviewed safe-harbor framework used across the industry - policy templates, security.txt files, and an open directory of programmes and their safe-harbour status. The project grew out of efforts by Bugcrowd, CipherLaw, Dropbox, Dr. Amit Elazari, NTIA and the US Department of Justice, among others, and now operates as a nonprofit. If you are choosing which programmes to trust, checking whether a programme publishes disclose.io-style terms is a fast quality signal.

US law moved too. In May 2022, the Department of Justice revised its Computer Fraud and Abuse Act charging policy to state that 'good-faith security research should not be charged' - defining it as testing done 'solely for purposes of good-faith testing, investigation, and/or correction of a security flaw', designed to avoid harm and used to promote security. It also drew the line the other way: claiming 'research' is not a free pass - finding holes in order to extort their owners is not good faith, whatever you call it.

Two honest caveats: safe harbour is a policy promise, not a magic shield - staying inside scope is what keeps it alive, and testing beyond the stated scope or causing an outage can leave you exposed. And none of this is legal advice; if you are relying on a programme's terms, read them yourself.

The law in plain words

Unauthorised access to computer systems is a crime in most jurisdictions - in the US under the Computer Fraud and Abuse Act, in the UK under the Computer Misuse Act 1990, and equivalents elsewhere. What makes research lawful is authorisation: the programme's scope and safe harbour are exactly that authorisation, in writing. Stay on named targets, respect prohibited actions, stop when asked, and report promptly. A researcher who wanders outside scope, or who keeps exploiting a bug after the report, can lose every protection the programme offered.

This is also why responsible disclosure beats full-disclosure shouting: publishing exploit details before a fix exists trades a few days of attention for real-world victims. The mature pattern is private report, agreed timeline, then public detail after the fix - 'coordinated disclosure'.

What a good report contains

Security teams triage dozens of reports; the actionable ones share a shape. This is the same craft as writing a usable pentest report, at smaller scale:

  • Reproduction steps - exact, numbered, from a clean state. If a reviewer cannot reproduce it in five minutes, it sits in the queue.
  • Impact - what an attacker gains: which accounts, which data, which privilege. Impact decides severity; see how CVSS scoring works if the programme uses it.
  • Suggested remediation - one paragraph. You do not have to write the patch, but naming the fix shows you understand the bug.
  • Evidence - screenshots or minimal proof-of-concept, with sensitive values redacted. Never exfiltrate real user data to prove a point.

Common bug classes - injection, broken access control, misconfiguration - are covered in web application security basics, and the worst-case end of the topic (bugs exploited before anyone reports them) is explained in our zero-day explainer.

For organisations: run one properly

If you ship software, you need a way for outsiders to tell you things are broken. The floor is cheap and worth it:

  • Publish a VDP - even without bounties. A security contact, a scope statement and a safe-harbour clause cost a page of text. BOD 20-01 shows what 'good' looks like.
  • Commit to a response time and meet it. Silence is what pushes researchers toward public posting.
  • Separate impact from novelty in triage, so researchers optimise for the right thing instead of the flashiest thing.
  • Credit reporters - a strong incentive that costs almost nothing.

And keep your own house in order first: the same attackers a programme attracts will walk through unpatched malware delivery paths you left open. Which systems matter, and which layer of security owns them, is the scoping question we unpack in cybersecurity vs information security.

FAQ

Is bug bounty the same as responsible disclosure?

They describe the same core activity - finding a flaw and privately telling the organisation - but a bug bounty adds payment and a formal programme. Responsible disclosure is the umbrella ethic; bounties are one incentive model inside it.

Do I need permission to test a website for bugs?

Yes. Only test targets covered by a published programme or written permission, stay inside its scope, and respect its prohibited actions. Without authorisation, testing is unauthorised access regardless of your intentions.

What protects me from legal trouble?

The programme's safe-harbour clause, and staying within its rules. In the US, the DOJ's 2022 CFAA policy directs prosecutors not to charge good-faith security research, but the protection depends on your conduct actually being good faith - within scope, no harm, reported to fix.

What should I do first if I find a vulnerability?

Stop testing, save minimal evidence, and find the organisation's security contact (a security.txt file, a VDP page, or a disclosure platform). Report privately with reproduction steps and impact, and agree a disclosure timeline before saying anything publicly.

Are bug bounties worth it for beginners?

They are a good forcing function once you can reliably find and reproduce real bugs - triage is strict and duplicates are common. Learn the foundations first: web application security, then report quality via our pentest report guide.
