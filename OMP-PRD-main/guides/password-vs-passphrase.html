Password vs Passphrase: Which to Use

For anything you must memorize, a passphrase wins; for everything else, a random password in a manager does. The comparison table and entropy math that settle it.

The quick answer

Use a passphrase (5–7 random words) for the few secrets you must memorize — your password manager's master password, device unlocks, disk encryption. Use a long random password from a generator for everything else. Passphrases buy length with memorability; random passwords buy maximum entropy per character. Together, they cover 100% of your accounts.

The entropy math in one breath

A 20-character truly-random password from a 95-character pool holds 20 × log₂95 ≈ 131 bits. Six words from a 1,296-word list hold 6 × log₂1296 ≈ 62 bits. The random password wins on raw bits — but 62 bits resists years of online guessing and slow-hash offline attacks, and the passphrase can actually be remembered and typed. Different tools for different jobs, not rivals. Deeper dive: How Password Entropy Works.

When to use which

  • Passphrase: manager master password, laptop password, FileVault/BitLocker keys, Wi-Fi you sometimes dictate aloud, answers to security questions (as word salad).
  • Random password: every website and app login, API-adjacent credentials, anything a manager can store and autofill.
  • Hybrid trick: memorable passphrase plus manager-generated for real sites — the master password is the only thing you ever type from memory.

Anatomy of a good passphrase (and a bad one)

  • Generated, not invented. "correct horse battery staple"-style phrases only work when words come from dice or a CSPRNG — humans pick correlated words (ilovemydog). Use the passphrase generator.
  • 5–7 words from a published list. Fewer than 5 from a 1,296-word list is too thin for offline attacks; more than 7 is diminishing returns.
  • Separators don't add real entropy — hyphens, spaces or none are about readability and site rules, not security.

Frequently asked questions

Is a passphrase just a long password?

Technically yes — but the word "passphrase" signals word-based construction, which is what makes it memorable. Systems treat them identically; the difference is in how humans produce and recall them.

Can passphrases be cracked with dictionary attacks?

Attackers absolutely try known-word combinations — that's exactly why the words must be randomly drawn from a big list. Six random words from 1,296 choices ≈ 2⁶² possibilities; human-picked "random-seeming" words collapse to a tiny, cracked-first subset.

Do spaces and capital letters make a passphrase stronger?

Barely, in entropy terms — they mainly help satisfy site rules. What matters is word count and random selection. We show entropy honestly (and what actually drives it) on the generator page.

Should my master password be a passphrase?

Yes — it's the canonical passphrase use case: maximum-strength secret you must recall without help. Six or seven generated words, written down once, stored physically safe until memorized.

Will passkeys make both obsolete?

For sign-ins, eventually yes — passkeys remove shared secrets entirely. But device unlocks, encryption keys and fallback passwords stay human-scale secrets for years, so choosing well still matters.
