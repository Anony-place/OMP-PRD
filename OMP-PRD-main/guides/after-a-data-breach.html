What to Do After a Data Breach

A calm, ordered playbook for the hours and days after a service you use loses your data — including password resets, sessions and scam watch.

Incident Response 7 min read Updated 2026-08-23
On this page
  • 1. Confirm what happened
  • 2. Change the right passwords first
  • 3. Kill sessions and tokens
  • 4. Harden the account
  • 5. Watch for follow-up scams
  • 6. If identity data leaked
  • 7. Reduce the next one's impact

A breach notice is not a reason to panic — it is a reason to act in the right order. Most damage after breaches comes from delay and from follow-up phishing, both of which you can control.

1. Confirm what happened

Read the provider's official statement (navigate to their site yourself — do not trust emailed links). Note what data was involved: passwords (hashed or plaintext?), card numbers, IDs, addresses, security answers. The response differs by data type.

2. Change the right passwords first

  1. The breached account's password — immediately.
  2. Every account that reused it (this is where a password manager's audit view shines).
  3. If your email was involved and weakly protected, secure it — email resets everything else.

Generate fresh, unique replacements with a random generator, not by tweaking the old one.

3. Kill sessions and tokens

Changing a password does not always sign out existing sessions or invalidate API tokens. Use "sign out of all devices", review connected apps, and revoke anything unfamiliar — especially on email, social and cloud accounts.

4. Harden the account

  • Enable or upgrade 2FA (app/key over SMS).
  • Refresh recovery emails/phones and security answers.
  • If the site supports passkeys, switch — passkeys give attackers nothing reusable to steal.

5. Watch for follow-up scams

Breach news is prime phishing season. Expect emails claiming to be the breached company, "support" calls, and refund scams. Golden rules: navigate manually, never share codes, and treat urgency as a red flag. See How Phishing Attacks Steal Passwords.

6. If identity data leaked

For government IDs, SSN-equivalents or payment cards: contact your bank, consider a credit freeze / fraud alert with your national bureaus, report card numbers for reissue, and document everything. Many jurisdictions give you free monitoring or remediation rights — check the official notice.

7. Reduce the next breach's impact

You cannot stop companies from being breached. You can make each breach boring: unique passwords everywhere, minimal data shared, passkeys where possible, and a manager keeping track. A breach of a site where you are unique-and-random is an inconvenience, not a crisis.
